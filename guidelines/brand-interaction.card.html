Motion 120-200ms ease-out, and none at all under reduced-motion. Hover may brighten a surface; it may never introduce semantic colour. The ring is --qm-focus-ring drawn outside the border box, so it survives a filled variant, and it is :focus-visible only — a pointer press leaves none behind, and a disabled control never takes it.
Under the ring, on the same band, sits --qm-focus-outline at --qm-focus-outline-offset: 2px of transparent outline, invisible here and doing two jobs. It displaces the browser's own indicator, which otherwise paints over the ring rather than instead of it — and it is the whole indicator under forced-colors, where box-shadow is dropped and a transparent outline is forced to a system colour. So a QM control never answers the browser's outline with none: that looks fixed and leaves a high-contrast user no focus indicator at all. Ship both, always.
Before any of these states there is reachability, because a state is only a state if something can be in it. Anything that answers a click is a control, and ships as one: a <button>, or the role, the tab stop and the Enter/Space handling that stand in for one where the markup cannot change. A div carrying an onClick shows no ring at all — not QM's and not the browser's — because nothing can focus it: that is a missing control, not a missing indicator. Semantics follow the handler both ways, so an element given none stays out of the tab order and is announced as nothing. And a set of them takes one tab stop, not one each: a tablist, a radio group and a listbox are entered once and traversed with arrows.
Which leaves the rule the other three serve: a focus ring means keyboard focus, and nothing else. Not decoration, not selection, not a way to draw the eye to the field a flow wants next — those have their own signals above, and the ring is spent if it also carries them. So nothing paints the focused look by hand: a well ringed because a consumer styled it that way is the unreachable div above in better clothes, promising a caret that never arrives. QM ships one exception and names it — focused on Field and PromptField, for a specimen picturing a focused well that holds no control at all. A gallery card may reach for it; a product surface may not. It overrides in both directions, and the direction that hides a real ring is the worse one. Everywhere a real control sits in the well, the well lights its own and the consumer keeps no focus state.

QM's position, so nobody has to infer it: destructive actions get <Button variant="destructive">, which carries cinnabar at rest. That is the rule above holding, not an exception to it — the semantic colour is already there, and hover only moves its value. A hover-only warning is no warning on keyboard or touch. It is tinted and outlined, never filled: cinnabar cannot carry legible label text at any weight. Confirmation remains the stronger pattern and QM prefers it wherever the surface can afford the step; the variant exists so that the cue is never simply dropped when it cannot.

A state has to be named before it can be entered, and a name the system does not know is the case behind all the others. One answer everywhere: the value resolves to the component's default in every respect — rest, hover, press, the glyph beside it, the label — and development says so once per distinct value in the console. Never a throw: a design system that crashes takes someone else's screen down over a chip colour. And never a partial resolution, which is what shipped — Button resolved an unrecognised variant to secondary's colours and then matched none of the nine hover branches keyed on the raw prop.
So the rule is where a branch reads from, not what the fallback is: every branch keys on the resolved row, never on the raw prop. Hover, the label and the dot handed to a child are columns beside the colours, because a second read of the prop is a second chance to disagree with the first. Two fallbacks are deliberately not the declared default: an unrecognised StateDot or TimelineRow state goes grey rather than to canon, and a RouteChip to unknown rather than to a count nobody took. A fallback may not assert canon and may not claim material. The types and the lint reject an unrecognised literal, so a value that gets this far was computed — the one value no tool here can see, and the whole reason the warning exists.
